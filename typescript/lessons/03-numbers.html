Numbers & Math

Scores, prices, positions in a game, the time on a clock: programs are full of numbers. TypeScript does math instantly, and its types make sure you only do math with real numbers.

The math operators

OperatorDoesExampleAnswer
+add7 + 29
-subtract7 - 25
*multiply7 * 214
/divide7 / 23.5
%remainder (left over)7 % 21
**power7 ** 249

The remainder % is surprisingly handy: n % 2 is 0 for even numbers and 1 for odd ones. For "how many whole times does it fit", use Math.floor(a / b).

Math follows the usual order: brackets first, then **, then * / %, then + -. So 2 + 3 * 4 is 14, and (2 + 3) * 4 is 20.

Text that looks like a number

When people type into a web page, you always get text, even if they type 42. TypeScript makes sure you convert it before doing math:

Two things to notice. TypeScript flags typed * 2, because multiplying text makes no sense. But typed + 1 is allowed, because + also joins text: the result is "421", which is probably a bug! When something can't be converted, you get NaN, a special number meaning "Not a Number".

A tiny surprise

0.1 + 0.2 gives 0.30000000000000004. Computers can't store some decimals exactly. It happens in every language. Use .toFixed(2) when you show money to people.

Common mistakes

  • Joining instead of adding: "5" + 3 is "53". Convert with Number() first.
  • Forgetting Math.: it's Math.round(x), not round(x).
  • toFixed gives text: (4.5).toFixed(2) is the string "4.50". Do your math first, then use toFixed to show it.

What is 17 % 5?

5 fits into 17 three times (15), with 2 left over.

Remember

  • + - * / % **. Brackets first.
  • x += 5, x++, x-- change a number.
  • Math.round floor ceil max min abs sqrt random. x.toFixed(2) for display.
  • Number("42") turns text into a number. NaN means it couldn't.
