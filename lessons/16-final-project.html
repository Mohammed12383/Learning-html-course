The finished website

Here's the whole project. It has two pages (index.html and about.html) and one shared stylesheet (style.css). Look around: click the menu, try the Phone size, hover over the tiles, send the form.

It might look like a lot of code. But every single line is something you've already learned. Let's walk through how it was built, one step at a time. Keep the playground above open and follow along in the code.

Step 1: plan before you code

Professionals don't start by typing. They start with a quick sketch: what sections the page has, and what goes in each one. You can do this on paper in two minutes.

navlogo, two links, a "Join" button
herobig headline and buttons on the left, a picture of the app on the right
featuresa heading and five tiles in a bento grid
quotea happy user with a profile picture
joinan email box and a button on a gradient
footercopyright and links

Then pick your style: fonts (Fredoka for headings, Figtree for text), a main color (pink #FF5C8A) and a few soft colors (pink, blue, mint, yellow, lilac).

Step 2: the skeleton and the fonts

From lessons 1, 6 and 13.

Every page starts with the skeleton. In the <head> there are two <link> tags: one loads the Google Fonts, and one loads our style.css.

Step 3: variables and basics

From lessons 4, 8 and 14.

The top of style.css saves every color as a variable. Change --main and the whole site changes color. Then come the basics every site needs: box-sizing, a font on the body, and pictures that never overflow.

The .container class is the "max-width + margin auto" trick from the Sizes lesson. Every section uses it, so all content lines up in a neat column, never wider than 1100px.

Why padding-left and padding-right?

Sections use both .container (side padding) and .section (top and bottom padding). If .section used the shorthand padding: 72px 0, the 0 would erase the side padding from .container. Writing each side separately keeps them from fighting.

Step 4: the navigation bar

From lessons 9 and 11.

The header is a flex container with three items: the logo, the links, and a button. justify-content: space-between pushes them apart. The links are their own small flex container. The "Join free" button reuses the .btn and .btn-main classes, the same ones the big buttons in the hero use.

Step 5: the hero

From lessons 6, 12 and 14.

The hero is a grid with two columns: text on the left, the app picture on the right (grid-template-columns: 1.1fr 1fr). The headline uses clamp() so it's big on laptops and still fits on phones. The yellow marker behind "beautifully" is a gradient that only fills the bottom 40% of the word.

The best part: the app picture isn't a picture at all. It's made of divs. A white rounded card, tilted with transform: rotate(2deg), holding four colored event rows. Two emoji float around it using a @keyframes animation. Turn on X-ray in the preview and point at it to see the boxes.

Step 6: the feature tiles

From lesson 12.

The features are a bento grid: repeat(3, 1fr) makes three columns, and the first tile has grid-column: span 2 so it's twice as wide. Each tile gets one soft color class (.pink, .sun, .blue...), which are written once and used by the event rows too. On hover, tiles lift up 6px.

Step 7: the quote and the sign-up form

From lessons 3, 10 and 14.

The quote uses <figure>, <blockquote> and <figcaption>, the right tags for a quote with a name. The avatar is a circle thanks to border-radius: 50%.

The sign-up form sits on a pink-to-blue gradient. The email box and button are in a flex row that wraps, so on a phone the button drops below the box. The input has an aria-label instead of a visible label: it's read aloud by screen readers. That's fine when the design has room for only one box, and the placeholder makes it obvious.

Step 8: phones and tablets

From lesson 15.

At the bottom of the CSS, two media queries change the layout:

  • 860px and below: the hero becomes one column (text on top, app picture below), and the bento grid becomes two columns.
  • 600px and below: the bento becomes one column, the nav links hide (they're also in the footer), and the sections get less space.

Finally, prefers-reduced-motion switches off the floating and hover animations for people who asked their device for less motion. Press Phone 375 under the preview to see it all.

Make it yours

Now the fun part. Turn Daybloom into your website. Ideas:

you

A personal site about you: your hobbies, your favorite things, a gallery.

a club

A page for your team, class or club, with a sign-up form.

a shop

A pretend shop or café with a menu and prices.

an app

A landing page for an app idea you'd love to exist.

Your turn: remix it

  1. New name and logo: change "daybloom" and the 🌼 in the nav and footer.
  2. New colors: change the variables at the top of style.css. Try --main: #5B6CFF;.
  3. New words: rewrite the headline, the lead text and every tile.
  4. New fonts: pick two from Google Fonts and update the link and the font-family lines.
  5. A new section: add a "How it works" section with three numbered steps.
  6. A new page: in the my-site folder, copy about.html to contact.html, change its content, and add it to the menu on every page.

Work on the real files

Open the my-site folder of this course in VS Code (File → Open Folder). It has the exact same files as the playground. Open index.html with Live Server, and you're building a real website on your own computer. When you're happy, put it online with Netlify Drop or GitHub Pages (see lesson 13).

What to learn next

You now know the HTML and CSS that real websites are built with. Here's where to go from here:

  1. Build three more small sites. Practice is everything. Copy the layout of a website you like (for practice only), then design your own.

  2. Play CSS games. Flexbox Froggy and Grid Garden are free browser games that make layout stick.

  3. Learn JavaScript, to make menus open, build quizzes and games, and save information. You've already seen a taste with onclick.

  4. Keep good references: MDN Web Docs (developer.mozilla.org) explains every tag and property, and web.dev/learn has free courses on CSS and design.

You want to change Daybloom's pink to blue everywhere. What's the fastest way?

That's the power of CSS variables: every part of the site uses var(--main), so changing it once changes everything.

You did it

When you started this course, a website was a mystery. Now you can plan one, build it with HTML, style it with CSS, lay it out with flexbox and grid, make it work on phones, add hover effects and animation, and put it online. That's real web development. Go build something you're proud of. 🌼
