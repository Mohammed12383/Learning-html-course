Phones & Tablets

More than half of all website visits happen on phones. A responsive website changes its layout to fit any screen: one column on a phone, several columns on a laptop. Here's how.

Why it matters

Phone390px
Tablet820px
Laptop1440px

A laptop is almost four times wider than a phone. A layout with three cards side by side looks great on a laptop, but on a phone each card would be squeezed to a thin strip. So we tell the browser: "on small screens, stack them instead".

In this lesson, use the Phone, Tablet and Laptop buttons under each preview to switch screen sizes. The current width is shown on the right.

Step 1: the viewport tag

Without this tag in the <head>, phones pretend to be a 980px-wide computer and shrink the whole page down until it's tiny. Always include it. (It's already in the VS Code ! skeleton.)

It means: "make the page as wide as the device's screen, and don't zoom".

Step 2: layouts that stretch

Many things you've already learned are responsive by themselves. Use them and most of your site will just work:

  • max-width instead of width for containers.
  • img { max-width: 100%; height: auto; } so pictures never stick out.
  • flex-wrap: wrap so rows break onto new lines.
  • repeat(auto-fit, minmax(200px, 1fr)) for grids that pick their own number of columns.
  • %, rem and vw instead of large fixed pixel sizes.

Step 3: media queries

Sometimes you need different rules for different screen sizes. A media query is a set of CSS rules that only works when a condition is true, like "the screen is 600px wide or less".

(max-width: 600px)the screen is 600px

Press Phone 375 and the cards stack. Press Laptop 1280 and they sit side by side. The rules inside @media (max-width: 600px) only apply on screens 600px wide or less. Everywhere else, the normal rules are used.

Where to switch: breakpoints

The width where your layout changes is called a breakpoint. You don't need many. These are the common ones:

BreakpointCatches
(max-width: 600px)Phones
(max-width: 900px)Phones and small tablets
(min-width: 1200px)Big screens only

Choose breakpoints by looking

Don't think "iPhone" or "iPad". Make the preview narrower and wider, and add a breakpoint wherever your design starts to look squeezed. That's the right number for your design.

max-width vs min-width

max-width

"This wide or smaller." Design for laptops first, then fix things for phones.

min-width

"This wide or bigger." Design for phones first, then add columns for bigger screens. Many pros prefer this ("mobile first").

Testing on your own computer

Chrome has a phone simulator built in:

  1. Open your page in Chrome and press F12 (or right-click → Inspect).

  2. Click the little phone-and-tablet icon at the top of the panel, or press Ctrl+Shift+M (Cmd+Shift+M on a Mac).

  3. Pick a phone from the list at the top, or drag the edges to any width.

And of course: open your site on your real phone too. Nothing beats testing with your own thumb.

Phone checklist

  • Nothing scrolls sideways.
  • Text is at least 16px and easy to read.
  • Buttons and links are big enough to tap (44px tall).
  • Pictures fit the screen.
  • Nothing important only works with hover.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the viewport meta tag. The page looks tiny on phones.
  • Fixed widths like width: 900px. Use max-width.
  • Media query in the wrong place. Put media queries at the end of your CSS, so they win over the normal rules above them.
  • A missing brace. A media query has its own { } around the rules, which have their own { } too.

When do the rules inside @media (max-width: 600px) apply?

max-width: 600px means "up to 600px", so phones and small screens.

Remember

  • Always include <meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1">.
  • Use max-width, flex-wrap, auto-fit grids and max-width: 100% images.
  • @media (max-width: 600px) { ... } for phone-only rules. Put them at the end.
  • font-size: clamp(min, vw, max) for titles that scale.
